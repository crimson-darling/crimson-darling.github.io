## I Won’t Miss My Mom Even a Little

I’ve been leaving for so long. 12 drawn-out months have passed since my return offer to the US, and I am disinterested in both my hometown and the mundane patterns I’ve fallen into inside of it. I gave into my mother’s pleas, extending my start date as far as HR would allow, but her tearful prayers have made the extra months feel increasingly airless. 

Your early twenties come preheated with expectation: work effortfully but selflessly to retire your caregivers, learn as much as that prefrontal cortex can squeeze in, travel because the money comes back but your mediocre range of mobility won’t. My early twenties are instead consumed by one unthinkable thing - an ungrateful daughter leaving the country that her immigrant parents suffered for.

<br> 

My mom routinely makes my bed, her mechanism for holding onto me during my last few months here. Not with comforters or cushions, but with nightly reminders of the nightmare I’ve become. “You’re so excited to leave us, aren’t you?” “This is all I’ve ever feared, our family being broken up and in different parts of the world.” “This is what I get for moving here and leaving Mama in Sri Lanka. This is God showing me how hard it must have been for her.’”

There was one time I experienced the graceful sensation of empathy. Maybe two, or three if I think back far enough. But I’ve been encircled by enablers who encourage my boundless aspirations, the only people my mom detests further than me. I can’t change the world from my bedroom in Pickering. 

I’ve tried relaying that she mustn’t take it personally. I recently shared my fear that this move was a major betrayal to my family. My dad was quick to shut down the claim, insisting on their pride. My mom bore silent, wandering eyes. 

She likes to remind me of my friends, cousins who would never abandon their parents. “Nuha is so close to her mother, she reminds me of myself.” “See how Hana got married but then moved back in with her mom? How Aunty Jolly doesn’t even need to call me anymore because her daughter keeps her so entertained?” “Some girls are naturally inclined to have love and closeness with their families. You are not one of those children, so you must contact us daily.” I think my mom just likes those daughters better because whatever they want from life has not required them to leave home.

<br> 

It’s been a very long time since she’s been employed. I think this is one of the largest distances between our understanding of the world. When my parents used to argue, my dad’s sharpest fighting words were that my mom doesn’t know the value of a dollar. Making one, spending one hundred, saving one thousand - foreign concepts to her. She has spent most of her adult life financially dependent on my father, while I have spent mine terrified of depending on anyone. I am a sculpture of who she could not be. 

And, inconveniently, of exactly who she was.

Because she, too, moved countries at the same age. Younger. I try to remind her that my behavior is no different, inspired even - a nation-to-nation migration for the promise of a prosperous future. The distinction, to her, seems obvious: she left her mother to build a family. I am leaving mine to build myself. She exchanged her job as a teacher in Sri Lanka for an arranged marriage in Toronto with my dad. Though she only held the position for a matter of months, she still clings to her ‘childhood education background’, the final remnants of independence. 

She describes that despite her meager salary, she would give her entire paycheck to her mother. I am proud of my mother’s altruism, and I understand that quitting to marry my father resulted in much heavier pockets for my grandmother. This realization opened the blackout curtains that shield my impression of my mother - her sole priority was the well-being of her mother. Though my father turned out to be the most exceptional man, my mother would have settled for mediocre as long as sanctity was promised for her household.

<br> 

Accordingly, my mom is most offended by my rejection of a job offer in Ontario, 40 kilometres from home instead of 4000. It paid considerably less, but her persuasions that I’d save on rent and car payments were not entirely wrong. The insult is that the wage would be livable regardless, and I picked the option separating me from my mother. Though I’m pursuing the extra hundred thousand to whittle down our mortgage, my decision still feels selfish, less respectable than hers, because my parents don’t need my support the way hers did. Even a five-figure job could have been enough to fulfil my obligation, sharing paycheck cuts over her home-cooked meals, but that wasn’t enough for me. 

Maybe I inherited her instinct to provide for a mother, but none of her ideas about what providing should look like.

It’s honestly respectable, the way she’s unwilling to feign delight even to her friends. I almost admire her adherence to the script, ‘it’s so sad, no? She’s the light of our house. It’s going to be empty without her.’ This, I cannot criticize. Such things are true.

<br> 

Despite my indignation, I have unsparing love for my mother. I will taste her affection in the ground curry powder dusting my dinner. I will feel her judgement rerouting me to the other hijab in my drawer that matches better. I will hear her considerate point of view in arguments before I even begin them. The love of a mother is rich, sometimes wickedly so, and it will permeate through borders. But I won’t miss her. 

Missing is to feel an absence, and she will be with me. Her virtues, sprinkled into pockets of my day, are a much more manageable quantity of her love. The distance hides the encroachment she doesn’t know how to control.

For the short three months I was away last summer, my self-talk reached highs it has since forgotten. Her commentary quieted, replaced by the unknown, airy permission to just be. Her endless criticisms were effective in shaping me into a disciplined, purposeful child, but I am an adult now. I know how to devise my own criticisms and correct them gently. 

My mother is begging me to let her stay an extra couple of weeks after moving me in. I think this is why she has not booked her tickets yet. I have let her down kindly, but she hopes I will change my mind, reminding me that I have a whole year after that to live by myself, and she is unlikely to visit again. It’s impressive, her unabashed optimism that in a mere twelve months, I will forgo my goals and remember that home is where I belong. Her argument itself reminds me why a few extra weeks, especially without my father as a buffer, would not be productive for either of us.

But I will see her, in three months and then maybe six, and I will love her with ferocity. I will lose track of time cuddling tightly and massaging her shoulders. I will fill her in on my new friendships and she will update me on her old. I will enjoy the flavours of my favourite meals and pack foil-wrapped portions for the flight back. She knows how to be a mother, she does. And she’ll do it even better from the other end of the continent.
