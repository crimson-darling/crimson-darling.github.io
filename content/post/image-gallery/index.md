The Halal:Haram Ratio

March 2, 2026 / Ramadan 13, 1447 AH

<br> 

Before beginning this essay, I fearfully checked for any Arabic-sounding names in my Workshop group. Just one - not ideal, but I’ll survive. Judgement from my own is the most tormenting.

My hijab introduces itself before I do, rightfully positioning Muslim as the first identity that people observe. I am also Aaliyah, a Technical Program Manager, a Creative Writer, a Certified Personal Trainer, a Nonprofit President, a daughter, a sister, a best friend, an athlete, etc. etc.

But as my hijab indicates, I’m a Muslim first.

This introduction leads to spoken and unspoken questions – peers curious about my morals and boundaries, how ‘Muslim’ I really am, what topics of conversation are safe, or how far I’m willing to go. Through each phase of my life, these answers have pivoted. And as it does each year, the holy month of Ramadan has arrived, forcing me to study what this phase of my life tastes like. Marinated through years of revelations and misconceptions, from myself and my company, I decided to reflect upon where exactly God and I are standing at the moment.

First page of my diary on January 2, 2017

<br> 
 
Fact: In elementary school, and early into high school, I used to enjoy probing my Muslim friends, ‘Are you religious?’

Belief: I liked to think I was God’s golden child. Sunday School was an instrument of my faith, so when I graduated the 10-year program, I reckoned I had completed Islam.

But more than anything, I believed. I well and truly believed. I knelt in prayer with unbarred trust that God would actualize my preteen dreams and extinguish my preteen problems. I know my friends didn’t have the kind of relationship with Him that I’d managed to build so young, but I wanted them to. I hoped, in each repetition of my query, that I would one day be met with ‘No, but I’d like to be. Could you teach me how?’ Nobody ever gave me the opportunity to make them better. To make them like me. To make them God’s golden children.

<br> 

**Fact:** I pray the 5-times-a-day mandatory prayers, alongside several extras. I stay seated longer than my friends to do the little post-prayer prayers, and I focus on my enunciation every word. 

**Belief:** The more time I spend talking to God, the more time He will spend marinating my wishes. My wishes specifically, though not necessarily at the expense of the others’.

<br> 

Fact: I used to have an alliterative saying I would patronize friends with, jokingly, though I meant it, that discouraged profanity. I would memorize countless rap songs, measuring my pauses between words to keep my mouth clean.

Belief: My intentionality with my tongue painted me as more righteous than my Muslim friends, who weren’t even aware of the ways they weren’t meeting my standards. It was admittedly short- lived, the pressures of high school company getting to me in the last year or two, but I cyclically (attempt to) return to this Listerine condition. I am currently in one of those cycles.

<br> 

Fact: During menstrual cycles, women are exempt from prayer.

Belief: Humorously, I believe God aligns my major events with my cycle, so I’m always painted on my big days. Since as early as 13, I can remember being on my period for every pivotal event in my life. I’d spend the week leading up to these occasions praying for success – to come first in the math contest, be selected as valedictorian, present a spoken word at the talent show. And when the day came, so would a familiar stream.

When not on our periods, and not exempt from prayer, Muslims are required to perform ablution, or wash their bodies to prepare – face, arms, feet; a quick process that can be done in the bathroom. We must tidy ourselves before speaking with God. Accordingly, wearing nail polish is not allowed, because water cannot get underneath the polish and clean your fingernails, so you are not fully tidy in front of God. I know – it feels like a bit of a technicality, and not particularly important, but I don’t mind the ruling. It has heightened the stakes of the landscapes on my fingertips – I only have one opportunity a month to deliver myself this deliberate art.

More seriously, I prophesize that due to the volume of prayers and work I put into these occasions, God rewards me with when the days arrive, absolving me of the requirement to step away from my mission 5 times for prayers. Though it’s not a burden either way.

<br> 

Fact: I have tried a little weed.
Belief: This is not haram; smoking is strongly disliked, but not forbidden. Well, that was my original belief – this meticulously crafted loophole I used to soothe myself with only applies to cigarettes, not marijuana. The Islamic prevention of intoxicants ensures one is always sober enough to complete prayer. I’ve only indulged a few times, but during none of those instances would I have been able to supplicate without a fit of giggles.

<br> 

Fact: I’ve kissed a boy. Or two. Or three. Or it doesn’t really matter how many, but much fewer than I can count on my two hands.
Belief: Until university, boys wouldn’t even look at me. My teeth were uneven stalactites in a cave that my braces were yet to industrialize, and my unyielding acne did not complement my underdeveloped sense of fashion. These are not criticisms; at age 13, I think you should be a little frumpy. But it makes sense that when prettier, grown-up me began attracting sweet fruits, and I wanted a bite of everything in the basket. That’s not to say there have been many men, but there have been many more temptations.

<br> 

Fact: Islamically, I shouldn’t be sharing any of this; we are encouraged to conceal inappropriate activities to ‘protect our dignity, allow for private repentance, and prevent the normalization of immorality in society’.
Belief: Though completely aligned with this rationale, I find that I connect to peers better – my Muslim peers included – due to the lived experiences I’ve cultivated. I prefer to be skilled in each endeavour I take on – and that requires practice! Is it shameful to develop talent? And then share that talent with my girlfriends, inspiring them? And besides, sharing lived experiences, of substances or sexy times, is the glue that melds young people together. I’d suffocate from the FOMO of not participating in such discussions.

<br> 

Fact: My friend Adam left Islam. He noted that Muslims don’t ask enough questions about what they believe in, but that is not necessarily a bad thing. He says they’re happier for it. I wish I could say we’re happier for it; I was taught that only Muslims go to Heaven, and I would love to see him there.
Belief: It’s common enough to hear of converts joining Islam, but rare to hear of Muslims leaving. I theorize it’s because many keep the name after losing their faith – a label easier to passively accept in tight-knit communities rather than scandalously abandon.

<br> 

Fact: Adam would intermittently ask me why I wear hijab. Not a trick question nor asked with malice, just a thought he’d repeatedly follow up on. My answers were increasingly defensive. Belief: Through my university years, I fell victim to the practices of the ungrateful Muslim, only talking to God when I needed something from him. Yes, I would do my prayers, but I didn’t mean anything by them. Yes, I would go to His home, but the Mosque didn’t feel sacred anymore. Yes, I would read the Quran, but only because my mother insisted. I was far enough past my Sunday School days to acknowledge that I, regrettably, had not completed Islam, and that I never would.
My value in God’s eyes would require constant work, a disappointment I hadn’t predicted. I began to fall off track spiritually, less and less passionate about the lifelong commitment without Sunday School propping me upright.

<br> 

Fact: Far too late into my life, I unlearned the notion that only Muslims go to heaven.
Belief: This is reassuring. Katherine and Amiya are probably better people than I am, and they deserve to be there just as much as I do, if not more.

<br> 

Fact: ‘Slut Strands’ are a slang term to describe two face-framing strands of hair left loose from a skier/snowboarder’s helmet.
Belief: Hijabis can have those, too. I used to quietly gloat in the neatness of my own draping, never letting a baby hair hang loose. Why put the scarf on, intended to conceal your endowments, and then bring them front and centre?
And then I saw a TikTok addressing this precisely, the eagerness of strangers to bash a woman’s interpretation of modesty. That ten-second rebuttal layered over my stance and glued itself there – God prefers an imperfect effort over no attempt at all. These girls were doing their best, and though it didn’t look like my best, God wasn’t giving me a medal for that observation.

<br> 

Fact: I’m afraid to be a parent. Not a mother; I have no fears there. But a parent.
Belief: My own pair raised my brother and I so perfectly in religion, and I will never be able to replicate that. Though I sometimes wandered off the stone path, my parents paved it perfectly, nurturing us with Sunday School worksheets and engaging stories of the Prophets while populating young Muslim friend groups for us. My dad founded a youth-based Muslim Nonprofit that his daughter would lead ten years later.
We were ushered into Quran classes and long nights of repentance at the mosque, balanced with the freedom to pray and recite at our own will. We knelt in family congregation a few times each week, and both of my parents maintained distinct, unobstructed relationships with God. I yearned to one day build my own. I want to instill that yearning into my own children. But I’m afraid if I don’t do the leg work now, I will never be able to.

<br> 

Fact: At just age 12, we visited Makkah, and the holy black cube that God calls home. A journey accepted by God is characterized by Him spraying cold water over your slate, wiping away your immoralities and completely renewing your Iman, or faith. Visiting at such a young age did not absolve me of as much as it did for my parents.
Belief: That is the most important vacation I’ve taken to date. I remember finding tears in my eyes, in awe by how deeply I could feel God’s presence in His residence. My middle school self was already experiencing a love for my faith that many don’t achieve in their lifetimes. By the time I reached my parents age, I couldn’t imagine to what extent it could grow!

<br> 

Fact: For 8 years, I have volunteered with Serendib Youth Canada, a Nonprofit organization focused on leadership through service in Muslim communities. In 2025, I was elected as President, the socio-religious trailblazer for ~100 students.
Belief: An aunty dropping her son off for an event raved about how I am the role model Muslim girl, the only one who dresses modestly and covers hijab properly. This perception of me was laughable – if this aunty knew what the manager of this event was doing behind closed doors, she would not have let her son out of the car. Muslim kids should not be doing the things I did.
Especially in the presence of other young Muslims, the self-imposed guilt pressed down heaviest upon my romantic history. Though I wasn’t privy to what they were doing behind closed doors either, it felt deceitful to be running initiatives in Mosques and kissing boys in cars. In an effort to relieve that shame, I successfully made it through the year with no improper interactions. Except for two slip-ups on January and December 31st, but the start and the end don’t count.

<br> 

Fact: I’ve done my share of looking for marriageable Muslim boys. It’s impossible to find ones who aren’t performative – they’re all the ‘Muslim by name’ kind. I use that term lightly because I’m unclear whether I fall into that bracket, too.
Belief: The reality is, I noticed how few Muslims I know actually move through this little life with deep-seated, unapologetic tawakkul – ‘perfect trust in God and reliance on Him alone.’ Maybe my trust in God is not perfect yet, but I am at least on my way there. The Muslim men in my circles – none of them are en route yet, which is not something I judge them for, but also not something I will hold their hand and help them find.

<br> 

Fact: I have a partner.
Belief: I use the word partner, and I call him my man, but I will call him nothing else. Boyfriend? No. Dating? We go on dates, but I wouldn’t say that. Relationship? I mean that is what you call a connection between any two people; when asked for an emergency contact, there is a blank for ‘Relationship’, and that does not exclusively apply to your partner.
These terms taste bitter to me, because of their implications; these are all undoubtedly Haram, or forbidden. And what I have with him? There is nothing Haram about that. People like him remind me of God’s existence, because the best way to describe my man is tawakkul personified. Every breath of his is rich and full of God consciousness, and his mere presence raises me in my faith.

<br> 

Fact: I did go through the depressive adolescent phase of sporadically wanting to kill myself. It was never serious until I took 9 Tylenols at once and started panicking about whether I would wake up the next morning. Though all I successfully managed was a stomach-ache, I was maybe a little more serious than most kids who go through that phase, but not by a large margin. And that’s because suicide is Haram.
As with all rulings, there are exceptions there, but the bottom line is that God decides when your time is up.
Belief: I hate to dignify my lacklustre attempt through its mention, but I didn’t know how ineffective it would be at the time. All I knew, the morning after, was that I was of sound enough mind that I wouldn’t fit any of the exceptions. This blasphemous act would prevent my parents from mourning their child in the way they deserved, and they’d be shunned by our Muslim community. So, I never attempted again. Religion has kept me alive, in the rawest sense of the word.

<br> 

Fact: I never craved for Him in times of mental illness, not even at the peaks. Self-harm, car crash, sexual assault – it wasn’t personal, but He wasn’t my go-to.
Belief: I know that God controls everything, and that everything happens for a good reason. But beyond ‘building my lore’, I’ve never wanted to consider the implications of why He would put me through certain scenarios, or rather, why He puts people all over the place in scenarios that are much more unforgiveable and perverted than mine. He has control over everything, the ability to tailor every angle of every joint of every human being, and we’re being presented the Files? I’m still working on coming to terms with this one.

<br> 

Fact: I haven’t used the word sin because it’s a jaw slackening, heavy word for me to pronounce. I wouldn’t ask you to pronounce it either.
Belief: I can comfortably admit that I have made mistakes, or wrongdoings, or immoral decisions, but I would not go as far as to call myself a sinner. A Muslim, a person, a girl who sins – even that is too stern, because I also do many other things, and wouldn’t tag myself as ‘a girl who does good deeds’.

<br> 

Fact: If you walk towards God, He will run towards you (Sahih Muslim 2687)
Belief: Ramadan is not easy – during the holiest month of the year for Muslims, we are bound to 30 days of intentional hunger, eliminating worldly desires, and long nights in prayer. And I mean long – one nightly prayer has 20 repetitions, lasting 1-2 hours, and then in the last 10 days, the second prayer involves standing from 2am-4am and asking God for forgiveness. These are not mandatory, and my appearances are sporadic, but my parents never miss a night.

Which exemplifies that it’s not easy, but certainly doable. My commitment to the month is non-linear – I have no problem cutting out music, movies, or TV shows, and even the fasting is quite manageable. I weightlift 6x a week, but now instead of during my morning gym shifts, I make a second visit to Pan Am late in the evenings, uninterrupted by my own thirst. But I’ve also continued seeing my partner, I’ve deprioritized my daily Quran recitations, and I’ve consistently chosen schoolwork over those nightly prayers due to poor time management.

Despite my inconsistencies, it stands as both a Fact and Belief - If I walk towards God, He will run towards me.

Last night was the first time in a week I picked up my Holy Quran and the Ramadan Planner my partner bought me. I recited confidently and felt a soft liquid beneath my skin, that dormant soul stirring, and it dripped from my tongue onto the pages. The pages of both books, because when I picked up my Ramadan Planner to note down which Quran verse I had just completed, a small miracle happened.

<br> 

6,236 verses in the entire Quran. But the one I had just finished appeared on my planner. Not only the unlikelihood of the verse, but also the relevance of its content, were a pair of reminders that there are 17 days left of Ramadan, and God, As-Sabr, the patient, is waiting.
Excerpt from my 2026 Ramadan Planner:
My reflections led me to the realization that the distance between God and I is ever changing, a moving pin across the map of this little life. Though the facts and beliefs I’ve internalized around religion are often muddled, I do quietly applaud myself for being self-aware.
It’s only a quiet applause because being self-aware, even about misconceptions or beliefs I’d like to rewrite, does not always directly lead to change. But thinking about them is the first step in the right direction. Alhamdulillah, all praise is to God, for giving me these feet to take it.
