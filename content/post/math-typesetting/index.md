## Driving School: Crash Course

My best friend hacked my online driving modules so I could skip through the 200 hours of intuitive road signs. The value of being a computer science student – he just clicked ‘Cmd+Option+I’ (Inspect Element) and deleted the lines of code with the slide timers.

Apparently, I skipped through too fast, because when the snow was heaving and our car was turning and two strobe lights unexpectedly locked with my eyes, I couldn’t remember whether the car going left had the right of way.

Startling my steering brother would’ve probably been the wrong move, especially in this blizzard, especially because I wasn’t sure, because I wasn’t paying attention to my modules. That debt of attentiveness would go unpaid.

<br>

By age 5, my brother could narrate the entire script of Cars. He could weave together the meaningless details of every scene; the colour of the rims of the fourth-place car, the number of the lap where The King spun out, the year Doc Hudson won his third Piston Cup. He was unusually attentive to the vehicular world.

This knowledge enhanced his authority in the real world; I could point to any moving object on the highway, and he’d tell me the engine specs. I’ve seen him swerve an SUV between dotted yellow lines with the elegance of a motorbike, and there is no intersection in Pickering nor Scarborough he’d need a map to find.

<br>

So, I didn’t startle him. I trusted his expertise behind the wheel. And I had never been in the driver’s seat. The eye of a wintery hurricane was an inappropriate place to debut my newfound knowledge.

As we took the green light as an invitation to turn left, the oncoming Nissan headed straight towards us. Because of the snow, our turn happened unusually slow, giving the Nissan enough time to watch us disregard the right-of-way rules, but not enough time to brake against the frictionless pavement.

<br>


The car crashed twice - once into the offender, once into the curb. I did flinch, I remember now – I braced my head in the seconds before, knowing my little brain was the most important thing to protect, knowing my life was about to be altered and this car could take my limbs, my lungs, my laughter, but I could not let it take my little brain.

My brother was equally silent, so only one voice pierced through our broken car.

*I’m in a field of dandelions, wishing on every one that*

I’ll never hear a Ruth B song without being taken back to that moment; I make this known to every passerby when the song plays.

<br>

My dad flew out of the country that day. It was the first time he’d left the three of us alone, and his parting words were a plead not to drive – the roads were unsafe. My mom echoed his words once the flight took off, but I needed to host my volunteer event, and my ever-loving brother promised to drive me safely.

The silence lasted seven seconds at most before my brother turned to ask if I was okay. It was a loaded question – my head was on straight and my heart had not stopped and he was still beside me. We were capable of having a conversation despite the airbag hissing into my right ear, and no thick red blood painted our rich black interior. So, to say I was not okay in that moment would have been dishonest.

But that’s what I said. I felt nothing broken in my body, and I knew my brother would be even safer because THE CAR HIT MY SIDE, and I was aware that these things can be the final chapter of people’s stories, but I was still not okay. And I only bring this up because the next 10 minutes I spent in the car, sitting feverishly by myself with my thin wrists fighting the dashboard and hoarseness lining my screams were the only 10 minutes I was allowed to Not Be Okay.

<br>

My brother was outside, aimlessly trying to understand what to do. He was barely 20, only a year older than I, and from what little I remember, the driving modules don’t go over all this. He re-entered the car after a brief intermission of problem solving, telling me to relax and be quiet.

My mom called, asking what was taking him so long to come home after dropping me off at my volunteer event. Though I was not really done with my own share of shrieking, my mom’s sobs snapped me back into a new, perpetual reality: I did not get to be the victim of this accident.

<br>

I soothed her over the phone, promising I was Okay, we would be home soon, I know dad said not to go out, but we are Okay, and she will be Okay, and yes, we would call our uncle because dad was not home to pick us up in this terrible weather and mom was not in the state of mind to, and yes, we were both alive, alhamdulillah, and everything was Okay.

Though I didn’t know much about car crashes, I was certain this would ruin the next little bit of my life.

<br>

My uncle and brother sorted things out at the Collision Centre. I called my best friend, the one who hacked my online driving modules so I could skip through the 200 hours of intuitive road signs, and I cried. I cried and I cried and I CRIED. I couldn’t breathe, I couldn’t speak, I could barely even cry. He encouraged me to drink some water, and he promised me it wasn’t my fault.

But he was not there, he was not me, he was not watching the intersection like a bored teenager skipping through another screen of directional guidance, eyes processing the image but never her brain. He did not see the car approaching and wrack his brain as quickly as he could to locate that traffic light diagram with a red X over it, or a slide with words reaffirming that it was not her right of way. He did not understand that she considered alerting her brother from angles 0 to 90 degrees counterclockwise, at every point in the turn he was making, because her memory was failing her and she did not want to scare him. And maybe he was right, it was not her fault they crashed, but she didn’t even fucking try to stop it.

What was the point of bracing to protect that little brain? It clearly wasn’t holding much.

<br>

I don’t blame him, to be clear. My best friend who hacked my online driving modules so I could skip through the 200 hours of intuitive road signs. The display of brilliance I thought I immortalized by overriding a life or death system– it painted me a damn fool. I am not angry at him; I am embarrassed at myself for seeking out such a non-solution. The insignificant volume of time saved by Inspecting Element became the root of timeless suffering.

<br>

On another note, apparently everyone asked about me at the volunteer event. Man.

<br>

There was a welcome party at home, aunts and uncles who visited to ensure my mom was Okay. I got home and hugged my mother – I mean that exactly as I said it. She did not hug me, but I hugged her, comforting her, being her solace, withholding my tears so hers could moisturize my arms, cold and dry from the blizzard, and I promised her I was Okay, but she did not care that I was really Okay, Okay on the inside, she just cared that I was Okay on the outside, because that was much more comprehensible for her to deal with, the real victim of this accident, the mother whose husband was out of the country, a temporary single mother who almost lost both her kids.

<br>

I called my two best friends and giggled with every unbruised fibre of my being, suppressing what I later learned to label as the most lacerating incident of my young years.

My next morning started in the gym, because no door-shattering car crash would interrupt my routine. I was discovered by a friend at the hip thrust machine, my powerful movements supplemented by tireless tears. He led me outside and held me in his arms and allowed me to Not Be Okay.

Upon his recommendation, I took a bus from the gym straight to the hospital. I don’t blame my mom for not suggesting this first – she was the victim, if you recall, so she was allowed to take my word that everything was Okay, and that I should just continue my routine.

I was diagnosed with a severe concussion.

<br>

The gym and university were off limits for five weeks – I was not to look at screens of any sorts, and I needed ample rest. As a high achiever with more commitments than I can count on my slender fingers, this would not fly, and I planned on dismissing the nurse’s sanctions.

Later that day, though, the thudding of my brain and sensitivity in my eyes deflated the belief I was bulletproof. I refused to surrender my routine for the next couple days, though, shedding more tears each half-hearted workout and inhaling exponential doses of Tylenol to counter the ache of staring at my laptop. I gave up the notion of normalcy and accepted the inevitable anguish. My mom, however, was dissatisfied with my lack of resilience, pushing me to get back into school as soon as possible. My brother was just fine, and for her sanity, she needed me to be as well. As a high performer under pressure, I accepted her feedback and expedited my own recovery.

<br>

It makes sense - why my friends didn’t take the accident seriously for a while. My head was pounding and my eyes were burning and heart was hurting but I promised my mom and my aunts and my uncles and my brother who was crying for the first time I could remember that I was OKAY. I am strong, independent, and successful in convincing people so.

They never came to visit. I pleaded, I wasn’t supposed to be looking at screens, I didn’t want to video call, I wanted to be held with the permission to cry. I tried words and private story posts; I composed direct text messages and aimless phone calls. The intensity of the incident was muted by the deafening detachment I shielded it with, my friends innocently interpreting my Okays in the exact tone they were delivered with.

<br>

However, I had one friend who it felt safe to Not Be Okay with. A whirlwind of intrigue, empathy, and attraction connected the cell towers from North York to Pickering every single night for those five weeks. It was a surprisingly new friendship, but we would later laugh that trauma is the finest adhesive. To date, the comfort I feel in his presence is tethered to the vulnerability the accident invited. So, some good things came out of it.

Like a different flavour of being Okay that I’ll soon be celebrating three years of. To navigate my cross-sections of pain - suppression, prioritizing my mom’s processing over my own, underdelivering on my commitments – I started slitting my wrists, a recurring addiction. Once healthy enough to return to school, my new friend noticed, and I discovered he’d prefer to be with someone who didn’t do all that. To impress him, I temporarily kicked the addiction, and that questionable decision has led me to celebrating 3 years clean! Without the accident, I wouldn’t have spent enough time with him to care about his preferences, so thank you, blizzard and Nissan.

<br>

My therapist insisted I reduce my courseload, but I refused to let go of even one subject. Halfway through the semester already, I was confident in redeeming myself with exam season. After several meetings and bottomless convincing, I did eventually agree to get Accessibility support. I just hate feeling helpless.

My friends at school grew to be of more use – graded assignments and completed study guides began populating at my fingertips, and if anything, this accident taught me that I had built myriad supportive communities. Though I did get caught by the Academic Integrity Office for one particular offense, I would say passing all 6 courses with a raging concussion is admirable.

<br>

I haven’t mentioned it yet because I don’t like to, but it was my brother’s fault. It really was. Not that pointing fingers is particularly productive, but the car was written off and our insurance shot up and I got brain damage while he walked away unscathed, able to return to his normal life while I have to sit at home and pretend to my mom that I’m Okay, that I do want to spend time with her, that I don’t resent her for pushing me to go back to school instead of resting.

I don’t have any resentment towards him. It was an accident, of course. That’s why they call it that. But the bitter part of me wishes he suffered more. Not physically, ever – I would not wish that upon anyone. But I think more empathy would make him a more forgivable character in this story. Sometimes I also think it’s because he’s a boy, how unaffected he seemed.

<br>

It happened the day before my first scheduled in-class driving lesson. The accident set me back two more months, but the fear took much longer to dissolve. Where normal G2 students take 10 driving lessons, I took 20. My parents did not let me drive by myself for 1 year after my successful driving exam, because I admitted to panicking a bit and losing control during the test.

One month from today marks 3 years, and it still haunts me all the time. As I’m driving, while watching movies, when writing in my diary. I could’ve died. It was worse for me, because I was the passenger, and I watched the car approaching, and they didn’t brake, and my brother didn’t brake, and I watched every second of it happening, and I knew it was about to happen, and I felt I had the power to stop it, and that is guilt that is so insurmountable to me no matter how much time passes.

<br>

But I really am Okay now. I couldn’t pinpoint exactly where or when I made the transition, but sometime between the accident and today, I grasped that lofty adjective, and I mean it. There will probably be another incident that obliterates what I think the word means and forces me to once again redefine in it in the context of my changed life, but I am well-prepared for that now.

<br>
